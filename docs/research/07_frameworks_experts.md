## (b) Consolidated findings

### What the evidence actually supports (ranked by how much I trust it)

1. **Specific, concrete, subject-clear text beats vague text** (Hopkins, Heaths, Ogilvy, Hormozi, Kane, Wiebe). Support: practitioner consensus plus Wiebe's tests; not an academic RCT, but the claim is low-risk and consistent. Good default.
2. **Curiosity gap works, modestly, and only when the gap is about something the viewer already cares about** (Loewenstein: gap must target a question already in mind; Banerjee & Urminsky: forward-referencing wording raised CTR, effects within roughly +-3% CTR per SD; "when curiosity gaps backfire" title from search result suggests vague gaps can hurt, but I could not read that paper). Honest caveat: Upworthy's later experience (decaying effect, Robertson et al.) shows novelty wears off; and Muller's "Type 2" clickbait provokes backlash.
3. **High-arousal emotion (awe, amusement, anger, anxiety) is associated with sharing; sadness is not** (Berger & Milkman, 6,956 NYT articles, plus tiny lab studies). Correlational, one outlet, 2008. For a dog brand: amusement/awe/pride are the usable arousal emotions; do not lean on sad/sentimental memorial content for shares. Note one conflicting data point: Upworthy headline words, sad words raised CLICKS while joy lowered them - click and share are different outcomes.
4. **Self-expression/identity is the strongest *category-specific* lever** (pet fashion study: attachment -> anthropomorphism -> purchase, moderated by self-expansion; owners buy for the pet as for themselves). Peer-reviewed abstract + trade commentary; no data on wording itself. It supports "my dog is part of me" copy over generic "cute dog shirt".
5. **Packaging must match the delivered video** (MrBeast memo, Joos, Muller). A hook that over-promises cuts retention. Practitioner but widely repeated by insiders; consistent with Muller's Type 1/2 split.
6. **Voice-of-customer wording (steal buyers' own phrases)** (Wiebe/CXL). Single-site tests, selectively reported. Plausible and cheap.
7. **Concreteness of a CTA beats vague CTA** (Copyhackers: "Show me outfits I'll love" +124% button clicks vs "Sign up now"; Hormozi "tell them exactly what happens next"). Only a handful of tests but striking and directionally consistent. Button copy is the CTA analog for reels.

### What is weaker than the popular summaries suggest

- **Scarcity/urgency**: Meta-analysis (416 effects) says scarcity cues generally raise purchase intent, strongest for supply-based (limited edition) and for hedonic items; but a systematic review notes null/negative results in large field data, mostly student/MTurk samples, and effects are bigger for unfamiliar brands (small brand = plausible upside). Fake urgency is the risk (Hormozi: use only if real). For POD: "limited colourway" is only believable if actually limited (e.g., a design that rotates out).
- **Loss aversion/loss framing**: debated. Gal & Rucker argue "real but not fundamental"; others still find it at small stakes. Gain vs loss framing has weak, context-dependent evidence; for a $30 shirt there is no strong reason to prefer a loss frame, and "don't miss out" copy is untested for this niche.
- **Priming**: the poster child (elderly walking) failed pre-registered replication (d ~ .02). Do NOT build copy strategy on subtle priming; treat "pre-suasion" claims as hypotheses.
- **Social norms/proof**: the towel-reuse field effect (~+33%) is real in that data but only the combined "most guests + you" message worked, single norms did nothing, sample was affluent single-site; nudges overall are ~1.4% on average (126 RCTs) not 8.7%. For reels: use real numbers/real reviews, expect small lift.
- **AIDA/PAS/BAB/FAB**: no empirical testing found at all (Smart Blogger says so implicitly; stats in sample ads unsourced). They are writing scaffolds, not validated methods. PAS (agitate) fits short video poorly and tends toward negativity.
- **Hook model (Eyal)**: observational, built for habit-forming apps; variable reward claims unsourced. Mostly irrelevant for a 10 s apparel reel except "trigger" idea (make the hook tie to a daily cue, i.e., Berger triggers).
- **Ogilvy "5x as many read the headline"**: iconic but 1960s print, unreplicated; use as "headline matters" not as a number.
- **Hormozi / Kane / MrBeast**: practitioner claims. No controlled evidence; but Kane's "test 70-100k variations" and Muller's "I can't predict winners; survey was lopsided" are both a warning: you must test.
- **Vendor "3-second hold", "60% muted", "~70% lost if payoff late"**: unsourced; treat as heuristics (opus.pro, vidIQ, Bytecap all admit no benchmarks; Bytecap says compare vs your own Shorts).

## (c) Concrete wording implications for a dog apparel brand

### Practical rules distilled
1. Hook text 5-8 words; one subject word (breed/dog mom/dog dad) plus one stakes/curiosity word. Two lines max, no filler words (Hormozi-style source; unverified but logical).
2. Name the target person inside the hook ("Corgi moms", "Lab dads"): it filters, builds identity, and gives 2nd-person relevance (info-gap "importance").
3. Open a question or a contradiction in frame 1; answer it by frame 8-10 (promise, then deliver).
4. Make the reel's content match the hook exactly.
5. Use specific details: breed names, colour names, real numbers.
6. CTA: one action, stated with outcome ("Show me the breed list"), 1-2 s end card, not a speech.
7. Any social proof, scarcity or number must be true.
8. Treat all of this as hypotheses to A/B across your own reels; Muller and Kane both stress that prediction fails.

### Interpretation for the user's constraints
- The 10 s reel only has room for one hook, one proof/payoff and one CTA; frameworks like PAS/AIDA are too long unless collapsed into: hook (frames 1-2), detail/proof (3-8), CTA (9-10).
- Strongest bets from evidence: specific + identity + a real curiosity gap + a precise CTA. Weakest bets: fake scarcity, priming tricks, loss-framed urgency, vague sentimental copy.
- Sources for pet-specific claims (pet attachment -> anthropomorphism -> purchase, self-expansion) are the best category-specific evidence I found; nothing found tests on-screen wording for dog apparel directly, so all hooks above need A/B testing on the brand's own account.
