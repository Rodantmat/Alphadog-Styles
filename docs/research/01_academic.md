## (b) Consolidated findings by theme

Overall caveat: the strongest causal evidence (randomised A/B tests of text only, with clicks as the outcome) is for news headlines. Sharing evidence is mostly lab or news articles. Only #13, #27, #29, #39 touch short video, and none test on-screen hook wording.

### 1. Emotion, arousal and sharing
- High-arousal emotion drives sharing; arousal mediated effect beta .58-.67 across 3 lab studies. Awe +1 SD = ~+30% odds of top list, anger ~+34%, sadness lowers (#1).
- Emotional ads were shared more than informational: field b=.81; actual sharing 70% vs 48% (#43). Crucially, emotional content ONLY kept purchase intent when the brand was integral to the emotional story (4.42 vs 3.58 and brand evaluation +0.96 vs +0.01).
- Emotional wording helps hedonic products and backfires for utilitarian ones (#44). Apparel with dog art is hedonic/expressive, so emotional wording is on the safe side.
- CONFLICT: positivity associated with virality in #1 (beta .13), but in headline clicks negative words raise CTR 2.3% per word and positive words cut it 1% (#49, #41). Reels analysis found neutral tone highest and positive lowest (#39, weak). Reconciliation: clicking on news (threat/negativity) differs from sharing warm identity/affection content (#32, #45). Do not import negativity bias into a pet-love brand; the negativity result is from news headlines on one outlet in 2013-15 and faded as novelty wore off (#49).
- Negative emojis outperformed positive ones in health tweets (#5); positive emoji ~ no emoji. Playful emoji placed before text helped brand tweets (#6). No short-video emoji evidence.

### 2. Curiosity gap and clickbait
- Curiosity-gap writing predicts clicks in some data (question and info-gap cues: #21, #2), but clickbait reduced shares ~48% (80.6 vs 155.7 average) and the penalty was fully mediated by perceived manipulation (#3). Omitted information itself had only marginal link to shares.
- Clickbait headlines reduced attention to the article body (0.37 -> 0.27 normalised fixation) (#7). Writers incentivised for attention wrote shorter, more !/? text and lowered accuracy (#8).
- Concreteness: a 2025 PMC paper on curiosity-gap backfire (PMC11704130) could not be fetched; not used. Banerjee & Urminsky find concreteness and numbers helpful (#41).
- Implication: a gap is fine if the payoff is delivered inside the 10 seconds; a bait that is not paid off is penalised.

### 3. Identity signalling and in-group sharing
- People share to express identity (most common reason, #47); identity-framed messages raised word-of-mouth intention (4.92 vs 4.28; 4.80 vs 4.30) even for non-experiencers (#17).
- Sharing is driven by the SENDER's own taste more than item quality (#15) and people avoid sharing what makes them look bad when broadcasting (#16).
- Disclosing hobbies produced the largest trust/helpfulness gain (+8.6 pts helpful votes, #18), supporting identity-specific cues (breed, hobby).
- Cues beat interest: environmental cues +15% WOM per SD, visible products 19% vs 2% mention rate; "interesting" did not predict ongoing talk (#19). Cue words such as breed names or situational triggers (walks, vet, park) should help recurring mention.
- Pet content spreads as a token of affection to friends and family (#32, conceptual only), and dog owners post happier content (#45).
- Narrowcast framing (one specific person) increases sharing of useful content (76.7% vs 61.4%) (#16): supports "send this to a person who..." CTAs.

### 4. Second-person address and self-referencing
- Supportive: "you" raised involvement and brand attitude (#10); "your" wording raised ownership and purchase intention for potential customers, no difference for existing ones (#11); second-person raised self-referencing only for concerned audiences (#9).
- Boundary conditions: lower effect for high-collectivism consumers (#10).
- CONFLICT: in 4,873 randomised headline tests, direct address to the reader was NEGATIVE for CTR (#41), while self-referencing question headlines had the highest click uplift (+175%, +257% on ads, #21). Likely explanation: relevance to a specifically concerned reader matters more than the pronoun itself (#9).
- Facebook pronoun study (#30) was withdrawn; ignore.

### 5. Question vs statement
- Questions out-clicked statements by 137-257% in real Twitter and classified-ad data (#21); one product reversed.
- Under high arousal, statements sell more; questions work better in low arousal (#22). A fast, high-energy 10 s reel is arguably a high-arousal context, so a plain declarative or a very short question may do better on conversion than clicks.
- Rhetorical questions work for concerned audiences only (#9).

### 6. Humor and playfulness
- Humorous/humanized TikToks scored higher on brand fun/stand-out and "inclined to buy" 87% (#20, low reliability). Playfulness mediates emoji benefit (#6). In TikTok text analysis, caption humor helped only when the video was a meme format (#12).
- Animal videos had the highest median short-video retention (#27), a structural advantage for dog content.

### 7. Text length, readability, specificity
- Easier-to-read posts earned more likes, comments, shares, with causal experiments (processing fluency) (#31). Attention-seeking authors shortened text by 7.5-13.3 words (#8).
- CONFLICT: longer, more complex tweets were more retweeted in COVID data (#37, #38), but this is news-like information content. Use #31 (consumer posts, causal) for brand copy.
- Numbers, concreteness and information-seeking cues have measurable but small headline effects (about +/-3% per SD) (#41).
- Informational captions beat emotional-support captions for views in the TikTok mental health set (#12); text title/description added modest predictive power for watch percentage (SRCC +.015) (#13).

### 8. On-screen text, captions, first seconds
- Short video viewing is front-loaded: ~24% of TikTok views end before 20% of the video, ~40% before 50% (#29); ~70% of Shorts sessions end before 20% in a controlled crowdsourced study (#27). Median attention 82% implies many views are full watches of short clips (#29).
- Native TikTok-style fonts got first fixation in ~380 ms vs ~720 ms for custom fonts; logo-prominent ads had shortest viewing 1.1-1.6 s (#35, n=20, low).
- Right-side text got more fixation than bottom text (#36); text position on a vertical phone untested.
- Subtitles alter attention patterns but did not change recall (#33). No quantified caption lift from peer-reviewed sources retrieved.
- Predictive models show visual/audio features carry most signal; text adds about 0.01-0.02 SRCC (#13), so wording is a refinement, not the main driver.

### 9. Hashtags
- Hashtag count vs likes: Pearson ~0.03 (#40, tiny sample); hashtags negatively associated with retweets (b=-.035, #38); generic "viral" hashtags (#fyp) had higher views in a TikTok sample but the gap shrinks over time (#46). Hashtags at tweet end is norm (69%, #26). Overall: weak, no strong support for many hashtags.

### 10. Call-to-action and purchase intent
- "Shop Now" beat "Learn More" on urgency (6.39 vs 6.10), impulsiveness and purchase intention (9.16 vs 8.74) (#23, thesis, low).
- Scarcity: limited QUANTITY beat limited TIME in two studies (3.43 vs 2.62; b .50 vs .19) (#50, #51) and drew more attention (#52); but a plain scarcity message did not raise intention in another (#53), whereas FOMO framing did (3.36 vs 2.63). Perceived scarcity -> impulse buying via FOMO (#55). All small, student or convenience samples.
- Explicit participation CTAs appear in TikTok creator videos (#48) but with no engagement metrics.
- Imperative language backfire paper (JRIM) could not be fetched.

### 11. Pet context
- Dog owners post happiest content (#45); pets as identity extension and political identity differences in purchases (#24); digital pebbling of pet content (#32).

## (c) Concrete wording implications for a dog apparel brand

Evidence strength is stated per item. Treat all as hypotheses to A/B test on your own posts.

1. Name the breed and the owner identity in the first line of on-screen text, and make it speak to the owner rather than describe the product. (Identity framing: #17, #47; hobby/identity disclosure: #18; cues beat interest: #19; relevant-to-concerned audience: #9, #21.) Example: "Border Collie people know." / "Corgi mom starter pack."
2. Use a specific, narrowcast send prompt rather than a generic "share": "Send this to the friend who owns a Frenchie." (Sharing to one person raises sharing of useful content 76.7% vs 61.4%, #16; identity expression is the main reason people share, #47.) Moderate confidence (lab).
3. Make the first on-screen line short, concrete and easy to read (about 5-8 words, plain words, a number or named breed if natural). (Readability causal effect #31; concreteness and numbers helpful #41; native-style fonts read faster #35, weak.) Avoid text that must be decoded.
4. Prefer a self-relevant question OR a clean declarative, and test both. Questions out-clicked statements in field tests (+137% to +257%, #21) but statements win when arousal is high (#22) and rhetorical questions only help people already concerned (#9). Suggested test pair: "Is your dog a Lab?" vs "Lab dad energy."
5. Emotional, affectionate, playful wording (love, pride, humour) is supported for hedonic products (#44, #43, #6). Keep the product visible as part of the emotional story (brand integral) so sharing does not come at the cost of purchase intent (#43). Do not copy the negativity-in-headlines pattern (#49): it comes from news, was one outlet, and positive pet content is the category norm (#45).
6. Do not bait without payoff. If the first frame teases ("wait for the last one"), the last frame must deliver, because perceived manipulation drove a ~48% share loss for clickbait (#3).
7. Use "you/your" for breed-specific lines targeted at prospective buyers ("your Lab's hoodie"), since possessive framing raised ownership and purchase intention for non-customers (#11), but do not rely on the pronoun alone (#41 found direct address negative in headlines).
8. CTA: use a direct, concrete action with quantity scarcity rather than time scarcity if the drop really is limited ("Only 50 Corgi hoodies"): quantity beat time in #50, #51, #52. "Shop now" over "Learn more" (#23, weak). Frame FOMO ("don't miss this breed") only if true (#53). Evidence is thin, student samples.
9. Keep captions short and plain; use few hashtags (breed + one or two category tags). No evidence that more hashtags helps (#40, #38); generic #fyp has fading advantage (#46).
10. Emoji: use sparingly and playfully, ideally before the text as a teaser (paw/dog emoji) (#6). Evidence is weak to mixed for positive emoji (#5 shows no advantage).
11. Put sound-off safe on-screen text in a native-looking font and avoid logo-first frames; logo-led ads had the shortest viewing times (#35, n=20, low reliability). Captions/subtitles change attention but quantified effects were not found in peer-reviewed sources here (#33).
12. Make the slideshow's text change cue-rich and recurring (breed names, situational triggers like walks, park, vet) to build repeat mention; visible, cue-linked products are mentioned 19% vs 2% (#19).
13. Dog animals are an advantage in retention (#27); do not waste the first 2 seconds on logo or intro, since 24% of views end before 20% of the video (#29).

## Gaps and what to test
- No retrieved peer-reviewed study tests on-screen hook wording for 10-second product reels, Pinterest video pins or Facebook Reels. Directly test: question vs declarative vs identity-callout hook; "send to" CTA vs none; quantity-scarcity vs none; emoji-first vs none; keep other variables fixed.
